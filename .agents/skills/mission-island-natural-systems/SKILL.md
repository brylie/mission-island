# Mission Island Natural Systems Reviewer

Think like a field naturalist and a simulation engineer. Start with one thing a player should be able to observe, such as fish nearest a wader scattering first or reeds clustering along a wet bank. Ask what local cues, physical limits, and feedback could produce that observation. Read [Generativity](../../../docs/game-design.md#generativity), the relevant code, and the [Workbench guide](../../../docs/workbench.md). Use [Island climates and seasons](../../../docs/design/climate-and-seasons.md) for environmental context. Ground species-specific claims in a field observation or a credible source and identify where the game simplifies them.

Use the shared [review workflow](../../../docs/playtests/review-workflow.md) to share seeds, routes and scenario starts. If a rule cannot be isolated or observed, identify the control or readout that would make the experiment repeatable.

## Model the cause at the right scale

- **Agents and groups.** Give an individual limited perception, a plausible reaction time and a movement budget. Distinguish separation, alignment and cohesion so each can be inspected or tuned. Let local spacing, obstacles and different response times shape how groups split, turn and reform. A temporary leader can help a scattered group regroup. Check what the individuals still decide for themselves. When all agents should react to the same instant, read neighbor state from the same simulation step. [Reynolds’s flocking model](https://www.red3d.com/cwr/boids/)
- **Motion and noise.** Choose randomness for what it does. For a heading offset that drifts smoothly and tends back toward zero, start with the existing [mean-reverting noise helper](../../../src/simulation/motion-noise.ts), which takes a correlation time in seconds and a target deviation. Keep physical constraints on the resulting motion because the noise can produce outliers. Give individuals independent variation and use a shared signal for a cause they really share, such as a gust. Review turn speed, acceleration, hysteresis near thresholds and behavior near obstacles. Inspect the same motion at different rendering frame rates and advance gameplay at its intended simulation step.
- **Spatial fields and fractal detail.** Start from the large shape and its causes. Let elevation, drainage, slope, exposure, and distance from water create trends. Use seeded, spatially coherent variation at a few visible scales to break up an edge or add texture. Set the scale range in metres and compare it at walking and island distance. On a coast, retain the bay, the beach access and a safe shallow shelf while varying inlets and shore transitions. Share the shape data wherever rendering, depth and traversal need to agree.
- **Habitats, seasons and feedback.** Use the island’s climate to set seasonal ranges. Let the shared calendar shift those conditions over time. Local moisture, shelter and light can determine where a change appears first. Test feedback loops across their relevant timescales. In a pasture, forage drops as animals graze and recovers over time. In woodland, cover can change who ventures out and who can see them. Check whether anything stays depleted, crowds into one corner, gets trapped at a boundary, or repeatedly switches state. Keep persistent landmarks and critical routes usable.

## Separate evidence of emergence from variation

1. Pick an observable outcome and a measure that helps distinguish causes. For schooling, compare neighbor spacing, response delay, turn rate, and time to regroup. For a wetland, check where cover and foraging activity move as the ground dries. Include what two players should actually notice.
2. Replay a known seed and route. Change one cause while holding seed, time and inputs fixed. Disable or reduce one interaction to see whether the observed group pattern depends on it. Then compare a small perturbation with several fresh seeds. Look for varied paths that still obey the same limits. Diverging paths alone show sensitivity to the tested change. A group pattern can be called emergent when the interactions explain it.
3. Compare the isolated result with a real site. Use the Biome Composer or Landscape Workbench to capture comparable runs. Exercise relevant boundaries such as shallow water, low obstacles, repeated disturbance, long quiet periods or a season change. For shared behavior, verify that two clients see the same meaningful event and a reconnect sees the current state. Measure larger populations before adding spatial indexing or more simulation detail.
4. Report the proposed cause, the observed outcome and the test that distinguished it. Share the seed, conditions, route or setup link, and the range that worked. Flag the seeds or conditions that failed and name the smallest next experiment. Keep tuning in the owning simulation or mechanic spec.

## Start with the fish schooling trial

Open the [fish schooling trial](../../../docs/workbench.md#fish-schooling-trial). Start with seed 125. Compare an undisturbed school, a flank approach, and a direct skiff crossing. Replay the recorded route with the same seed and use the existing 5 cm nudge to probe sensitivity. Watch which fish react first, whether a U-turn is smooth, and whether groups reform without returning to a fixed formation. If a response looks synchronized, inspect local alarm, delayed leader following, and wander in isolation. Check the surviving change at Driftwood Beach with one player on shore and one in the water. The offshore depth and the second player's approach can reveal failures the studio pool hides.
