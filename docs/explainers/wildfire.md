# Wildfire

**Real world: draft. On the island: playable**, see [Fire watch](../mechanics/fire-watch.md). Last tended 2026-11-22. Model purpose: **illustrate** fire as a crew job, not predict spread.

Wildfire is fire burning through vegetation outside anyone's control. On a cool, wet island it can seem unlikely, yet moorland and woodland fires are a familiar spring hazard in places like the Scottish Highlands. This explainer covers why fires start and spread, how people fight them, why land is sometimes burned on purpose, and how ground recovers. It then compares the island's fires with the real thing.

## What a fire needs

A fire needs three things at once: **fuel**, **heat** and **oxygen**, the *fire triangle*. Take any one away and it goes out:
- Water cools.
- A beater smothers, cutting off oxygen.
- A firebreak removes fuel.

How a fire *behaves* once burning depends on a second set of three: **fuel**, **weather** and **topography**.

- **Fuel.** Fine, dry, dead material catches and carries fire fastest: grass, dead bracken, heather. Thick wood burns slowly. The amount, dryness and continuity of fuel decide whether a fire can keep moving.
- **Weather.** Wind pushes flames onto unburnt fuel and feeds them oxygen. Sun and low humidity dry fuel out. Rain soaks it.
- **Topography.** Fire runs faster uphill, because the flames lean over and pre-heat the slope above them.

## Why spring, on a wet island

Many upland fires in the UK and Ireland happen in spring, not high summer. Over winter, last year's grass, bracken and heather die back and dry out, but this year's green growth hasn't started. A few dry, breezy days in March or April can leave hillsides covered in tinder. Once the new growth comes through, the green leaves hold water and the ground is harder to burn.

## Kinds of fire

- **Surface fire.** Burns along the ground through grass, heather, bracken and litter. Most moorland fires are surface fires.
- **Crown fire.** Climbs into tree crowns and runs through the canopy. It is dangerous, and much harder to stop.
- **Ground (peat) fire.** Smoulders down into dry peat, sometimes for weeks, and can flare up again after a surface fire seems out.

Mature Scots pines survive many surface fires because their thick lower bark insulates the living wood. Young pines, with thin bark, usually don't.

## Fighting a fire

Crews work from the edges. The safest place to start is the *anchor point*: an edge of the fire that is already burnt or can't spread, such as a road or loch shore. From there they work along the flanks toward the head, the fast-moving front downwind. Common tools:
- **Beaters.** Flat flaps on long poles, to smother flames in heather and grass.
- **Water or backpack sprayers** for hotspots.
- **Firebreaks.** Strips cleared of fuel.

Fires are spotted by people watching from high ground, from towers or by aircraft. Each position is reported as a bearing, so others can locate the fire on a map. The island's Fire Watch tower and fire finder do exactly this.

## Burning on purpose: muirburn

On many heather moors, land managers burn small patches deliberately. This is called *muirburn*. Burning a patch in winter or early spring removes old, woody heather, so young shoots can grow. Those shoots feed grouse, deer and sheep, and the patchwork of burns of different ages creates habitat. The patches also act as firebreaks against a larger wildfire.

Muirburn is also controversial. Burning on deep peat can damage the peat, which stores carbon and water, and an escaped fire can become a wildfire. In Scotland, the Wildlife Management and Muirburn (Scotland) Act 2024 made muirburn a licensed activity:
- **Licence.** NatureScot issues the licences.
- **Peat.** Stricter rules apply on peat 40 cm or deeper.
- **Season.** Burning is allowed only in a defined season.

Muirburn is a good example of fire as an institution, not only an event: a practice with rules, permits, skills and arguments about how land should be managed.

## After the fire

Burnt ground recovers in a broad order:

1. **Ash and bare ground.** Exposed soil can wash away on slopes in heavy rain.
2. **First regrowth**, within weeks to a season. Bracken and grasses resprout from underground stems and roots that the fire didn't reach. Heather resprouts from the bases of young plants, and its seeds germinate after burning.
3. **Pioneers.** Birch seeds blow in, and young heath and scrub fill in over a few years.
4. **Trees.** Scots pine returns where there are seed trees nearby and grazing allows seedlings to grow. That takes decades.

A small, patchy fire can increase variety. A large, hot or repeated fire can strip ground back to bare peat and stone.

## On the island

The island's fires are a crew job: spot the smoke, fix the position, bring the right equipment, put it out together. See [Fire watch](../mechanics/fire-watch.md) for the rules.

- **Where.** Fires start only in six dry, grassy places, in daylight, and only a while after the last rain. After more than 12 dry hours a fire comes sooner.
- **Fire finder.** The crew finds a fire with the brass fire finder in the Fire Watch tower, which gives a bearing and distance, or by smoke seen from high ground.
- **Spread.** A fire is a cluster of *hotspots*. Each heats up, and once one is burning hard, the fire jumps to an unburnt hotspot nearby, usually downwind. Heavy rain cools them all and stops the spread.
- **Putting it out.** Extinguishers from the red fire stations; two crewmates work twice as fast.
- **Burnt ground.** Ash until the next dawn, shoots after the first dawn, grass and flowers after the third.

## Where the island differs

| Real | On the island | Why we simplified | What a player might wrongly learn |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fire spreads continuously through fuel, faster uphill and with wind. | Fire jumps between a few fixed hotspots, downwind; slope has no effect. | A handful of hotspots gives a readable job for two players and costs little to simulate and share over the network. | That slope doesn't matter. In reality, uphill runs are among the most dangerous. |
| Fuel type and dryness vary across a site. | Every fire site is "dry grass". Rain is the only fuel-moisture rule. | Keeps fire sites few and predictable while the mechanic is new. | That heather, bracken and woodland burn alike. |
| Crews use beaters, firebreaks and water, working from an anchor point. | Crews use extinguishers on each hotspot. | Spraying is easy to learn and works with the island's existing tools. | That extinguishers are the main tool for wildfires. In reality, beaters and firebreaks matter more on moorland. |
| Recovery takes seasons to decades. | Scars recover over three dawns. | Players see the change within a play session. | How fast land really recovers. The game compresses time on purpose, as the [learning brief](../design/learning-and-careers.md) allows, but should say so. |
| Crown and peat fires exist. | Only surface fires on grass. | Out of scope for now. | That a fire is always out once the flames stop. |
