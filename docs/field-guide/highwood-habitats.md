# Highwood habitats

**Status: planned. Last tended 2026-11-22.** The Highwood isn't on the island yet. These cards describe the habitats the placement work will build.

**New here?** Read the pinewood interior and the exposed ridge first: they're the Highwood's two extremes, and every other habitat sits between them. For processes that cross every habitat, see the explainers on [wildfire](../explainers/wildfire.md), [bracken slopes](../explainers/bracken-slopes.md) and [wet woodland](../explainers/wet-woodland.md).

The Highwood is the planned north-west backcountry of Starter Island: a mosaic of native pinewood, heath, wet woodland and an exposed ridge, with a fire access road and the Fire Watch on a summit. Its real-world models are the native pinewoods of the Scottish Highlands, such as Glen Affric and Abernethy. In places like those, soil, water, slope and wind decide what grows where, and one habitat grades into the next.

Each card follows the same shape:
- **What you see**
- **Why here**
- **What changes it**
- **Life**, which is the real world
- **In the game**, which is what a player will see

Plants are described in [Highwood plants](highwood-plants.md), and terms in *italics* are in the [glossary](glossary.md).

## Pinewood interior

- **What you see.** Tall Scots pines with bare lower trunks and separate crowns, with occasional birch and rowan. Light reaches the ground in patches over a carpet of bilberry, heather, moss and fallen needles.
- **Why here.** Poor, acid, free-draining soil, and enough shelter for tall trees.
- **What changes it.** Gaps open when old trees fall, and young pines grow up in the light. Old trees, *snags* and *deadfall* are a normal part of a healthy pinewood.
- **Life (real).** In Caledonian pinewood: red squirrel, crossbill, crested tit, wood ants and capercaillie.
- **In the game.** Tall pines, some old and broken-topped, with birch and rowan among them and bilberry beneath. Young trees appear only in the gaps.

## Edges and gaps

- **What you see.** Young pines and birches, bracken, juniper and a brighter, more varied ground layer where the canopy opens. This includes clearings, the fire road, and the ground around old windthrow.
- **Why here.** The extra light lets *pioneer species* in, and the ground is often disturbed.
- **What changes it.** *Succession*: birch comes first, and pine grows up through it over decades.
- **Life (real).** Edges are often the richest part of a wood: woodland birds feed in the light, butterflies use sunny clearings, and deer browse young growth.
- **In the game.** Saplings, bracken patches and fallen logs gather in clearings and along the road.

## Heath and juniper scrub (Juniper Hollow)

- **What you see.** Open ground of heather and grasses, with scattered juniper. Juniper grows as columns where it's sheltered and as spreading bushes elsewhere, with birch saplings at the edges.
- **Why here.** Thin, acid soils and grazing keep the ground open.
- **What changes it.** Without some grazing or disturbance, birch and pine slowly invade. With too much grazing, young juniper is eaten and old bushes are never replaced.
- **Life (real).** Common juniper has declined in many places because it needs open, lightly grazed ground for its seedlings. Heath supports moths, bees, ground-nesting birds and lizards.
- **In the game.** Heather in the open and juniper in both upright and spreading forms. The hares and foxes of Juniper Hollow use the cover.

## Exposed ridge (toward Windstone Ridge)

- **What you see.** Sparse, wind-bent pines that lean and stream away from the prevailing wind, with low juniper and short heather among bare rock.
- **Why here.** Wind dries and damages shoots on the windward side, so trees grow short and one-sided. This is *wind flagging*.
- **What changes it.** Storms throw shallow-rooted trees, so the ridge collects more deadfall.
- **Life (real).** Exposed heath and rock support specialists of thin soils and wind, such as mosses, lichens and low-growing heath plants. Birds of open hillsides, like meadow pipits, nest in the short cover.
- **In the game.** A few wind-bent pines all leaning the same way, with low juniper and heather, and no broadleaf trees or bracken.

## Wet woodland (Mirror Pool and stream banks)

- **What you see.** Alder and grey willow along water and in soggy hollows, with mosses, sedges and rushes beneath.
- **Why here.** Waterlogged soil, which pines can't tolerate. See the [wet woodland explainer](../explainers/wet-woodland.md).
- **What changes it.** The water level. Wet woodland on permanently waterlogged ground is called *carr*.
- **Life (real).** Alder fixes nitrogen with bacteria in its root nodules, and enriches the soil. Siskins feed on alder seeds, otters den among alder roots, and damp deadwood feeds many insects.
- **In the game.** Alder and grey willow by the water, with no pine.

## Bracken slopes

- **What you see.** Dense bracken on deeper, well-drained hillsides: green in summer, bronze in autumn, and flattened brown in winter.
- **Why here.** Deep soil and light. Bracken spreads underground by *rhizomes* and shades out smaller plants. See the [bracken slopes explainer](../explainers/bracken-slopes.md).
- **What changes it.** Grazing, cutting and fire. Bracken spreads where management eases off.
- **Life (real).** Bracken is poisonous to livestock, and its stands are where people often pick up ticks. Some birds, such as the whinchat, nest in bracken, and some butterflies breed where its litter shelters violets.
- **In the game.** Bracken patches on gentle slopes and in clearings. You can walk through them.
