# Forest roads

**Type: explanation (practice). Real world: draft, sourced, not yet checked by a person. On the island: playable**, see [the fire road](../../backcountry.md#fire-road). Last tended 2026-11-23. Model purpose: **illustrate** how a road is fitted to hilly ground. The game models the road's line, height and earthworks. It doesn't model soil, water, drainage or wear.

A forest road lets the vehicles a job needs reach the places it needs to reach, in most weather, without washing away. Fire crews, foresters, rangers and farmers depend on them. Most of what makes one look the way it does comes from four limits:
- how steep a loaded vehicle can climb;
- how tightly it can turn;
- how much earth has to be moved;
- where the rain goes.

Highwood is Caledonian pinewood in character, so this page leans on Scottish and UK guidance: the Scottish Government's Forestry Grant Scheme and the Forestry Commission's Operations Note 25, which is written for England. Where it uses the FAO's international field manual, that manual draws largely on North American research. Source ids in brackets, like (`scot-fgs-forest-road`), point to entries in the [sources register](../../sources.yaml). Each entry has a short quote from the page it came from.

## What the job is

A forest road is laid out on the ground, its line and height are fixed, earth is cut and filled to carry it, and stone is laid to make a running surface. Then water is led off it, and it is kept up for as long as it is used. Each part of that is a trade-off between cost, the vehicles, and the ground.

### Grade

**Grade** is how steeply a road climbs: the rise divided by the horizontal distance, as a percentage. A 10% grade climbs 10 m for every 100 m measured across the map, not along the sloping road. That is a definition, not a sourced claim.

Scottish grant rules for a new forest road set a maximum gradient of "8 per cent preferred, up to 10 per cent acceptable", and up to 12.5% on short lengths under 200 m (`scot-fgs-forest-road`). The Forestry Commission's English guidance says a forest road, usually built for 44-tonne articulated lorries, has a "Gradient not to exceed 10%", while a tractor or forwarder track may reach a 20% maximum but is usually 10–15% (`fc-on025-forest-roads-tracks`). These are standards for timber haulage. A road for lighter vehicles can be steeper. The same Scottish page sets a minimum gradient of 2%, except over short crests and sags, and the FAO manual uses the same 2% on level ground to stop water ponding (`scot-fgs-forest-road`, `fao-road-design-watersheds`). A road that is too flat drains as badly as one that is too steep.

To climb a hill steeper than the road may be, the road is made longer: across the slope rather than straight up. A **switchback** is the hairpin turn at the end of each traverse. The tightest bend a road can have depends on the vehicles that use it: "Minimum curve radius is vehicle dependent" (`fao-road-design-watersheds`). The English guidance gives a tractor track bends of at least 6 m radius and a forwarder track at least 8 m (`fc-on025-forest-roads-tracks`). It also says a track "should avoid hairpin bends, especially on steep slopes", so a switchback is a last resort, not a feature (`fc-on025-forest-roads-tracks`).

### Where the line goes

Before a road is drawn, someone walks the ground. The English guidance lists what the field survey records, including "rock outcrops, marshy ground and soft soil", water courses and other wet areas, old walls and buildings, veteran trees, and important wildlife habitat (`fc-on025-forest-roads-tracks`). The road "should avoid wet areas" and "avoid unnecessary travel up and down slopes" (`fc-on025-forest-roads-tracks`). So a real road bends on flat ground too: around a bog, an outcrop, a stand of old trees, a stream or a boundary.

The same guidance also says to "avoid long straight routes, especially along external boundaries", for the road's appearance in the landscape (`fc-on025-forest-roads-tracks`). A straight line is the shortest, but it isn't always the best road.

### Cut and fill

Most of a road's cost is moving earth. On a side slope, earth dug from the uphill side (the **cut**) is pushed out to the downhill side (the **fill**). The FAO manual says the road engineer's usual goal is "to balance earthwork so that the volume of fill equals the volume of cut", allowing for the way loosened earth bulks up or settles (`fao-road-design-watersheds`). A balanced road needs no earth carted in or away.

The slopes left beside the road are called **batters**. This page writes their steepness in the form defined in [conventions](../../conventions.md#space): horizontal distance, then vertical.
- **Fills** are loose spoil, so they can't stand as steep as the natural ground. The FAO manual says fill slopes can be built up to 36° to 38°, but common practice keeps them to about 34°, which it gives as a rise-to-run ratio of 1:1.5. That is 1.5 horizontal : 1 vertical (`fao-road-design-watersheds`). Fill slopes are also weaker: "Fill slopes are more succeptible to erosion and sloughing than cut slopes" (`fao-road-design-watersheds`, misspelling in the original).
- **Cuts** in firm ground can stand steeper. How steep depends on the soil or rock, and the FAO manual gives charts by soil type (`fao-road-design-watersheds`). A single typical figure for cut banks is still unsourced.

**Full bench.** On a steep side slope a fill has nowhere to sit. The FAO manual says that "when sideslopes exceed 50 - 55 percent", or the slope is unstable, the road may need **full bench** construction: the whole width is cut into the hill, and the dug material "must be end hauled to a safe location" instead of being pushed over the edge (`fao-road-design-watersheds`).

### Layers

A forest road is built in layers:
- **Formation.** The shaped natural ground, or subgrade, that the road sits on. The English guidance says the formation "should be shaped to mirror the final running surface", and that a track should be founded on natural ground rather than on fill wherever possible (`fc-on025-forest-roads-tracks`).
- **Capping layer.** Crushed stone laid on the formation across the full running width, to protect it from wear (`fc-on025-forest-roads-tracks`). Its depth depends on how weak the ground is.
- **Running surface.** The top layer of finer stone that vehicles drive on. On clay, "a finer top layer is then needed to seal the surface well" (`fc-on025-forest-roads-tracks`).

Scottish grant rules ask for "durable stone that will not readily break down under wheel loading", because stone that breaks down can cause diffuse pollution: fine silt washed off into ditches and streams (`scot-fgs-forest-road`).

### Water

The FAO manual says drainage "is of paramount importance in road design and cannot be overemphasized". It lists cut and fill failures, road surface erosion and weakened subgrades as "all products of inadequate or poorly designed drainage" (`fao-road-drainage-watersheds`). Water is what destroys forest roads.

**Shedding rain sideways.** The road surface is shaped so rain runs off it before it can run far along it. The FAO manual gives three shapes, measured as **crossfall**, the slope across the road (`fao-road-drainage-watersheds`):
- **Crowned:** higher in the middle, falling at 3 to 10 percent to each side. Crowned roads shed water fastest, because water has only half the width to cross.
- **Outsloped:** tilted at 3 to 5 percent toward the downhill edge, so water runs straight off. Outsloped roads are "simple to build and to maintain".
- **Insloped:** tilted at 3 to 5 percent toward a ditch at the foot of the cut bank.

The FAO manual's section on road surfacing gives a tighter crown of 3 to 5 cm per metre of half-width, which is 3–5% (`fao-road-design-watersheds`).

**Ditches.** Scottish grant rules require a roadside ditch on the uphill side, or on both sides where the road is at or below the ground beside it. The ditch must be at least 150 mm below the formation edge, with a fall of at least 2% (`scot-fgs-forest-road`). "Ditches and drains shall not lead directly into watercourses" (`scot-fgs-forest-road`).

**Getting water across the road.** Water in a ditch, or running down a long grade, has to be let across the road at intervals:
- **Culverts** are pipes under the road.
- **Rolling dips**, which the FAO manual calls intercepting dips, are broad dips that a vehicle drives through, sloping out to let water off. They are cheaper to keep than open-top culverts, but "their usefulness is limited to road grades less than 10 percent" (`fao-road-drainage-watersheds`).

Ruts make all of this harder: "In time, traffic will cause ruts to form, channeling surface water longitudinally down the road" (`fao-road-drainage-watersheds`).

**Ponding.** Where a road crosses a hollow on a fill, water running down the slope can be trapped behind it. The FAO manual's example says water "will pond above the road fill", which can weaken or erode the subgrade (`fao-road-drainage-watersheds`). That is why a fill across a hollow needs a culvert through it.

### Fords

Where a stream crosses a road and is usually low, a **ford** carries the road through the water. The FAO manual says fords suit "areas subject to flash floods", seasonal storm peaks or heavy debris, which "will simply wash over the road structure" (`fao-road-drainage-watersheds`). The crossing has to be armoured: "The fill surface has to be hardened either by concrete or large rock" (`fao-road-drainage-watersheds`). Roads "should climb away from channel crossings in both directions wherever practical", so high water won't flow along the road (`fao-road-drainage-watersheds`).

> **If you meet a flooded ford or road (safety review pending).** Don't walk, cycle or drive into flood water. The Scottish Government's Ready Scotland guidance says "Do not drive through flood water" and "Do not walk or cycle through standing or flowing flood water" (`ready-scot-rain-flooding`). The Scottish Fire and Rescue Service says "Do not enter into floodwater", warns that "Even a few inches of moving water can knock you off your feet" and that floodwater can hide sharp debris, branches and rocks, and says "In an emergency always call 999" (`sfrs-floodwater-2023`). Turn back and find another way. This box describes what a member of the public should do. It isn't training. A person still has to check it before the page is verified.

### Junctions

Where a forest road meets a public road, it widens into a **bell-mouth**: a flared mouth that lets a long lorry turn in or out. In Scotland a grant-funded bell-mouth is surfaced to "full highway standard", with bitumen macadam "base and surfacing layers" (`scot-fgs-bellmouth`). "Road junctions should be right angles where conditions permit", and vegetation must be kept back from the sight lines (`scot-fgs-bellmouth`). The English guidance adds that on a busy road a lorry turning left shouldn't have to cross the public road's centreline, and that "Infilling the turning corner" helps it turn (`fc-on025-forest-roads-tracks`). The size of a typical bell-mouth is still unsourced.

## Skills and kit

- **Reading the ground.** Seeing where the rock, bog, springs and steep faces are, and where the road can hold its grade. This is the field survey above.
- **Setting out.** Pegging the centreline and taking a **long section**, a profile of ground and road height along the line, and cross sections across it. The English guidance asks for cross-sectional drawings "showing gradients and crossfalls" (`fc-on025-forest-roads-tracks`).
- **Machines.** The English guidance calls a large tracked excavator "Ideal for cutting forest roads", and says a bulldozer, with no back-acter, is unable to lay culverts or grade batters (`fc-on025-forest-roads-tracks`).
- **Choosing stone.** Durable, well-graded stone for the capping and running surface (`scot-fgs-forest-road`).
- **Stabilising slopes.** Seeding and planting batters soon after they are cut. See *What goes wrong*.

## What goes wrong

- **Fills erode first.** The FAO manual says surface erosion on disturbed soils such as road fills "is highest immediately following disturbance and decreases rapidly over time", so slopes must be stabilised during and straight after construction (`fao-road-slope-protection-watersheds`).
- **Fills slump.** Seepage at the foot of a fill can cause a "Slump fill failure", and the eroding toe lets the slump work back into the road (`fao-road-design-watersheds`).
- **Steep batters stay bare.** Slopes steeper than 40 degrees "are often difficult to revegetate" (`fao-road-slope-protection-watersheds`). The English guidance says gentle batters vegetate more easily than steep faces, which "can remain as scars in the landscape for many years" (`fc-on025-forest-roads-tracks`).
- **Ruts become gullies.** Water runs in the ruts and down the road. The FAO manual reports that rutted surfaces yield about twice the sediment of unrutted ones (`fao-road-design-watersheds`). On clay, wheeled machines working the formation "may sink and create huge ruts" (`fc-on025-forest-roads-tracks`).
- **Wear.** Ruts, potholes, adverse camber on bends and settling wheel tracks develop, and the surface needs "Recarpetting" from time to time to replace lost stone (`fc-on025-forest-roads-tracks`).
- **Water with nowhere to go.** Ponded water above a fill, and blocked culverts or ditches, weaken the road from below (`fao-road-drainage-watersheds`).
- **Plants move in.** Grass and young woody growth spread onto the carriageway, and the English guidance describes herbicide spraying and flail mowing to keep them down (`fc-on025-forest-roads-tracks`). That an unused road greens over in the strip between the ruts first is still unsourced.

## How it's organised

- **Who designs it.** The English guidance says surfaced forest roads "should therefore be designed by a civil engineer", and recommends an engineer for large culverts and major watercourse crossings too (`fc-on025-forest-roads-tracks`). The guidance itself was written "to support woodland owners and managers in England". In the FAO manual the person balancing the earthwork is "the road engineer" (`fao-road-design-watersheds`).
- **Who pays.** In Scotland the Forestry Grant Scheme pays for new forest roads and bell-mouths into small, unmanaged woodlands. Its minimum road specification "is based on the Forestry Commission Standard Road Specification" (`scot-fgs-forest-road`).
- **Permission.** A forest road can need consent under the Forestry Environmental Impact Assessment (Scotland) Regulations 2017. "Any groundwork adjoining a public road (A, B, or C categories) and up to 25 metres from the public road will require planning permission" (`scot-fgs-forest-road`, `scot-fgs-bellmouth`). New forestry tracks need prior notification to the local planning authority (`scot-fgs-forest-road`). Tree felling needs its own permission.
- **Who keeps it up.** The English guidance covers road repair, recarpetting, and ditch and culvert maintenance, and says routine maintenance "should be determined by need and not necessarily by time span" (`fc-on025-forest-roads-tracks`). Who does the work, the owner's own staff or a contractor, varies. That part is still unsourced.

## Where the island differs

| Real | On the island | Why we simplified | What a player might wrongly learn |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Drainage is the core of road design: ditches on the uphill side, culverts under the road, rolling dips across it, and a minimum 2% fall so water never stands (`fao-road-drainage-watersheds`, `scot-fgs-forest-road`). | No ditches, culverts or dips, and no minimum grade. The island has no running water on its roads. | Water flow isn't simulated, and ditches and culverts need their own models. | That a road is just a graded strip of ground, and water doesn't matter. |
| A road is built in layers: formation, capping stone, running surface (`fc-on025-forest-roads-tracks`). | One graded ground surface with a tinted texture. | Nothing on the island sinks or wears, so layers wouldn't show. | That a forest road is bare earth. |
| Roads wear: ruts deepen, potholes form, stone is lost and has to be replaced (`fao-road-drainage-watersheds`, `fc-on025-forest-roads-tracks`). | The road never changes. Its ruts are painted on. | Wear needs traffic and weather simulation. | That a road, once built, lasts forever with no upkeep. |
| A forest road meets a public road at a bell-mouth, at right angles where possible (`scot-fgs-bellmouth`). | Junctions are graded as one network, with no flared mouth. | Not built yet. Bell-mouths are planned. | That a lorry can turn into a road as narrow as itself. |

**Real patterns the model misses:**
- surface water, ponding above fills, and the washouts that follow;
- what stone is used, and where it comes from;
- the landscape and planning side: grant specifications, consent, and appearance;
- erosion right after construction, and the planting that stops it.

## Notice this

Walk the Cairn Summit spur and stop where the road turns back on itself. Look at the slope above and below. Why could the road not go straight up?

Then look for where rain would go. On a real road, where would you dig a ditch, and where would the water cross?
